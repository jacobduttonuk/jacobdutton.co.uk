PROFILE

I get AI working for large companies, groups and portfolios: choosing where it pays, proving it on their own work and getting people using it every week. I’ve spent eighteen years helping large organisations change how they work and how they decide what to fund. With AI, that comes down to three questions: where it will save or make the most money (value creation), where it can grow what a company already sells (growth), and what new AI businesses are worth building (innovation).

Most recently I led innovation, R&D and emerging technology across ADQ’s 20+ operating companies. I own the value side of AI adoption: which use cases get funded, the proof before they scale, getting people using them and the business case. I work with the CIO, CTO and CISO on platforms, data, security and model risk, and nothing gets funded that can’t clear their bar. The work runs on whatever fits the job: predictive models, computer vision and physical AI, generative AI and LLMs, and agentic workflows connected to a company’s own systems through MCP, running headless with evals, guardrails and a person signing off where it matters.

How I work: fund AI like a portfolio, not an IT project. Plenty of small bets, money released in tranches, and every bet has to prove itself on the company’s own work to get the next one. For AI in what a company already runs: owner, baseline, accuracy target, supervised, then in use and measured on cost per correct outcome. For something new built with AI: demand, delivery, numbers, then scale.
Where this ends up. As AI pushes the cost of thinking towards zero, value moves from labour to capital and from the company to the portfolio: investors will ask how much of a company’s work agents can do and who keeps the margin. When every company runs the same models, the edge is what only it has: its data, customers, distribution and record of what worked. And customers will buy more through their own agents, so products need to be readable by machines and services paid for on outcomes.
