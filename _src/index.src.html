Fund AI like a portfolio, not an IT project.

Most companies fund AI like an IT project: pick the use case, build it, roll it out and hope people use it. That’s why so much of it stalls after the pilot.

AI behaves more like a portfolio of bets. You need plenty of small ones because most won’t pay off, and the money should follow the evidence. I release funding in tranches, and every bet has to prove itself to get the next one. What counts as proof depends on the bet.

Where this ends up.

Over the next decade AI pushes the cost of thinking towards zero. That changes who makes the money, and how.

Investors

Value moves from labour to capital, and from the company to the portfolio.

As agents take on more of the work, what a business earns depends less on headcount and more on what it owns: its data, its customers and its right to act on them. The diligence question becomes how much of a company’s work agents can do, and who keeps the margin when they do. Owners who fund AI across their companies as one portfolio, and share what works between them, will compound. Owners who leave each company to work it out alone won’t.

Companies

When everyone runs the same models, thinking stops being an edge.

What’s left is what only you have: your own data, the context of your customers, your distribution and the record of what worked. The companies that wire that into their agents, with clear rules on what agents can decide alone, pull away. The rest end up running the same strategy as their competitors, just faster. Org charts get flatter, and more of the company starts to run like a fund.

Customers

Customers will buy through agents, so products have to be ready for them.

More of the buying gets done by a customer’s own agent, comparing, choosing and reordering on their behalf. Products and services have to be readable by machines, through APIs and MCP, as well as by people. Services move from being sold by the hour or the seat to being paid for on outcomes, and brand counts for less in the search and more in the trust.
